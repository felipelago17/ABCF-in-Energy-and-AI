# Transaction Cost Economics of ABCF

**Summary.** Transaction cost economics (TCE), following Coase and Williamson,
asks *how* economic activity should be organised — through **markets**,
**hierarchies**, or **hybrids** — given the costs of transacting. Applied to
ABCF, it reframes corruption prevention as an **organisational-design and
investment problem**: how much to spend on which controls, and whether to govern
a given risk through internal hierarchy (in-house compliance), the market
(external vendors/certifications), or a hybrid (oversight boards, monitors,
JVs). This is the antidote to "compliance theater" — spending calibrated to
risk, not to appearance.

## Core TCE concepts, mapped to ABCF

| TCE concept | Meaning | ABCF manifestation |
|---|---|---|
| **Bounded rationality** | Actors can't foresee everything | Contracts can't enumerate every corruption pathway |
| **Opportunism** | Self-interest with guile | Bribery, kickbacks, self-dealing, hold-up |
| **Asset specificity** | Investments with little value outside the relationship | Sunk project capital that creates hold-up leverage |
| **Information asymmetry** | One side knows more | Intermediaries obscure where money goes |
| **Governance structure** | Market / hierarchy / hybrid | Vendor vetting / in-house compliance / oversight boards |

## The central question: when does prevention pay?

Prevention is not free. Due diligence, monitoring, controls, and oversight all
consume real resources. TCE gives a disciplined way to decide **how much**:

> Invest in a control up to the point where the **marginal cost of the control**
> equals the **marginal reduction in expected loss** it produces — where
> expected loss = probability of a corruption event × its full cost (penalties,
> disgorgement, monitor costs, debarment, reputational and financing costs, plus
> the opportunity cost of the tainted deal unwinding).

The corollary is that **controls should be unevenly distributed**. A
low-value, low-risk domestic purchase does not warrant the diligence a
high-value intermediary engagement in a high-risk jurisdiction does. Uniform
controls waste resources on low-risk transactions *and* under-protect
high-risk ones. The [ABCF Risk Matrix](../risk-tools/abcf-risk-matrix.md)
operationalises this calibration.

## Governance-structure choice

For any recurring ABCF risk, there are three ways to govern it:

1. **Market (buy):** rely on external signals and vendors — third-party
   screening services, ISO 37001 certification, external due-diligence
   providers. *Cheap and scalable; weak where the risk is relationship-specific
   or the signal is gameable.*
2. **Hierarchy (make):** internalise via an in-house compliance function,
   controls, and audit. *Strong control and information; costly and only
   justified where risk is high and recurring.*
3. **Hybrid:** oversight boards, JV compliance committees, external monitors,
   co-signature and audit-rights arrangements. *Fits intermediate, relationship-
   specific risks — the JV and large-project sweet spot.*

The choice is driven by **asset specificity** and **frequency**: the more
specific and recurring the risk, the further you move from market toward
hierarchy/hybrid governance. This is Williamson's discriminating alignment,
applied to compliance.

## Third-party due diligence as an asset-specificity problem

Third-party intermediaries are where most bribery risk lives, and TCE explains
why they are hard to govern:
- An intermediary relationship often requires **relationship-specific
  investment** (local knowledge, licences, government relationships) that the
  principal cannot easily replicate or verify.
- That specificity creates **information asymmetry** and **hold-up leverage** —
  the intermediary can extract rents or route funds opaquely.
- The governance response is **graduated**: light-touch market screening for
  low-specificity vendors; contractual controls (anti-corruption
  reps/warranties, audit rights, right to terminate) for medium risk;
  hierarchical oversight (direct approval, monitoring, co-signature) for
  high-specificity, high-risk intermediaries.

## Opportunism and hold-up in JVs and energy projects

Energy projects concentrate every TCE risk factor: enormous **asset
specificity** (sunk, immobile capital), **long duration** (decades of contract
performance), **state counterparties** (whose employees are foreign officials),
and **multi-party ownership** (diffuse monitoring incentives). The result is
classic **hold-up**: once capital is sunk, a counterparty or official can extract
side-payments because exit is prohibitively costly.

Governance responses that TCE predicts (and that the case record confirms):
- ex-ante **structuring** to reduce specificity and preserve exit options;
- **hybrid oversight** (JV compliance committees, reserved matters, audit
  rights) proportioned to the sunk stakes;
- **credible external monitoring** to substitute for absent trust.

## The efficiency test (against compliance theater)

TCE supplies a critical test any control should pass:

- **Does it change the expected-loss calculus, or only appearances?** A control
  that generates paperwork without shifting detection probability is theater.
- **Is it aligned to the risk's specificity and frequency?** High-touch
  hierarchy on trivial transactions is waste; market screening on bet-the-
  company intermediaries is negligence.
- **Who bears the residual risk, and are they the least-cost avoider?** Push
  detection to the actor best placed to detect (the polycentric move) —
  contractual audit rights and reps shift it to the party with the information.
