# Polycentric Governance in ABCF

**Summary.** "Polycentric" governance — Elinor Ostrom's term for systems with
**many centres of decision-making that are formally independent but functionally
interdependent** — describes how anti-bribery, corruption, and fraud prevention
actually works. No single actor "solves" corruption. Detection and deterrence
are produced by an overlapping network of public enforcers, private
gatekeepers, market participants, and internal governance bodies, each with
different information, incentives, and remedies. Prevention design is the work of
arranging those centres so their coverage overlaps where risk is highest.

## Why corruption resists monocentric control

A single central enforcer faces three structural limits:
1. **Information asymmetry.** Bribes are concealed by the parties who benefit;
   the enforcer is the last to know.
2. **Jurisdictional limits.** Corruption in a UAE energy JV may be invisible to,
   or unreachable by, any one national authority.
3. **Capacity limits.** No agency can audit every transaction.

Polycentric systems address these by **distributing detection** to actors who
are *closer to the information* and by **overlapping jurisdictions** so that a
gap in one centre is covered by another.

## The centres (and what each contributes)

| Centre | Type | Information it holds | Remedy / lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| DOJ / SEC | Public (US) | Cross-firm patterns, whistleblower tips | Criminal / civil penalties, monitors |
| SFO / FCA | Public (UK) | UK-nexus conduct, regulated-firm data | DPAs, regulatory sanctions |
| DFSA / FSRA, UAE authorities | Public (UAE) | Local & free-zone activity | Criminal & regulatory action |
| Corporate boards / audit committees | Internal | Direct operational visibility | Controls, discipline, resourcing |
| External auditors | Private gatekeeper | Financial-statement anomalies | Qualified opinions, escalation |
| Compliance function | Internal | Transaction-level red flags | Blocking, escalation, remediation |
| Institutional investors | Market | Aggregate governance quality | Capital allocation, stewardship |
| Whistleblowers | Insider | Ground truth | Reports (often bounty-incentivised) |
| Journalists / civil society (EITI, TI) | Civil | Cross-border, revenue-flow data | Exposure, reputational cost |

## The design principle: overlap where risk concentrates

The polycentric insight is not "more actors is better" — it is **targeted
redundancy**. High-risk interfaces (state-owned-enterprise procurement,
third-party intermediaries in high-CPI-risk jurisdictions, JV payment flows)
should be covered by *multiple* centres, so that failure of any one does not
create an undetected pathway. Low-risk interfaces need less overlap. This is the
transaction-cost logic applied to governance architecture — see
[`transaction-cost-economics.md`](transaction-cost-economics.md).

## How enforcers deliberately recruit private centres

Modern anti-corruption enforcement is engineered to *expand* the network:
- **Voluntary self-disclosure and cooperation credit** convert companies and
  their counsel into detection agents (report-or-pay-more).
- **Whistleblower bounties** (SEC) monetise insider information.
- **Corporate monitors** graft external oversight onto a firm post-settlement.
- **The FCPA accounting provisions** turn audit into anti-corruption
  surveillance.
- **Section 7 of the UK Bribery Act** makes the company itself the enforcer of
  its associated persons — the only defence is its own program.

Each mechanism *shifts detection cost outward* onto actors better placed to
detect. This is the central governance move: not more policing, but a better
**distribution of who watches what**.

## Coordination across centres

Overlapping jurisdictions require coordination to avoid both gaps and
double-jeopardy inefficiency. In practice this happens through:
- **Anti-piling-on** policies that credit penalties paid to other authorities;
- **Joint/coordinated resolutions** (e.g., UK/US/Brazil in Rolls-Royce and
  Glencore) — see [`../case-studies/uk-bribery-prosecutions.md`](../case-studies/uk-bribery-prosecutions.md);
- **Mutual legal assistance** and asset-recovery cooperation under UNCAC;
- **Information-sharing** among regulators and standard-setters.

See [`../governance-resources/enforcement-coordination.md`](../governance-resources/enforcement-coordination.md).

## Failure modes (where polycentric systems break)

- **Coverage gaps** — a high-risk interface watched by no centre (classic in
  minority-stake JVs where no partner has both visibility and incentive).
- **Diffusion of responsibility** — every centre assumes another is watching.
- **Captured centres** — a gatekeeper (auditor, local agent) co-opted by the
  bribe scheme.
- **Incentive misalignment** — a centre with visibility but no reason to act
  (e.g., a partner profiting from the tainted contract).
