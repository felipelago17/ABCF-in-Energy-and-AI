# Enforcement Authority Coordination

**Summary.** ABCF enforcement is shared among many authorities with overlapping
jurisdiction. Without coordination, that overlap produces two pathologies:
**gaps** (each authority assumes another is acting) and **piling-on** (a company
paying multiple times for one course of conduct). Coordination mechanisms —
joint resolutions, penalty-crediting, information-sharing, and mutual legal
assistance — are what make a polycentric enforcement system efficient rather
than merely crowded.

## The coordinating relationships

### DOJ ↔ SEC (United States)

The FCPA's two enforcers split the work: **DOJ** (criminal, plus civil
anti-bribery over domestic concerns and foreign nationals) and **SEC** (civil,
over issuers, especially the accounting provisions). They routinely resolve the
same matter in parallel, allocating criminal penalties, disgorgement, and civil
penalties between them (see the split resolutions in
[`../case-studies/fcpa-energy.md`](../case-studies/fcpa-energy.md) and
[`../case-studies/fcpa-ai-technology.md`](../case-studies/fcpa-ai-technology.md)).

### SFO ↔ FCA (United Kingdom)

The **SFO** prosecutes serious bribery/corruption (its principal tool being the
**DPA**), while the **FCA** pursues regulated firms for systems-and-controls
failures. Two authorities, two standards of proof, two remedy sets — a designed
redundancy that closes gaps a single authority would leave (see
[`../frameworks/uk-bribery-act.md`](../frameworks/uk-bribery-act.md)).

### Cross-border coordination

Major matters increasingly resolve **across jurisdictions simultaneously**:
- **Rolls-Royce (2017)** — coordinated UK (SFO), US (DOJ), and Brazil (MPF).
- **Glencore (2022)** — coordinated US, UK (SFO), and Brazil.
- **Société Générale (2018)** — US (DOJ, CFTC) and France (PNF).

These illustrate a mature coordination practice: parallel investigations, shared
factual records, and allocated penalties. (Figures and details in the
[case studies](../case-studies/README.md).)

## Anti-piling-on / penalty crediting

To prevent disproportionate cumulative punishment for one course of conduct, US
policy (the DOJ policy on **coordination of corporate resolution penalties**)
directs prosecutors to consider and **credit** penalties paid to other
authorities — domestic and foreign. This makes cross-border coordination
*rational* for companies (cooperate once, globally) and for authorities (avoid
redundant punishment that undermines legitimacy). It is the enforcement-side
complement to the compliance-side unification argued in
[`../compliance/sanctions-abcf-alignment.md`](../compliance/sanctions-abcf-alignment.md).

## The cross-border toolkit

- **Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs)** — formal requests for evidence and
  testimony across borders.
- **UNCAC cooperation and asset-recovery** provisions — the treaty basis for
  returning proceeds of corruption (see
  [`multi-stakeholder-initiatives.md`](multi-stakeholder-initiatives.md)).
- **INTERPOL** financial-crime and anti-corruption cooperation — coordination,
  notices, and network support among police authorities.
- **Joint investigation teams and information-sharing arrangements** among
  regulators and prosecutors.
- **Sanctions/export coordination** — OFAC/BIS actions increasingly run
  alongside corruption resolutions (Weatherford 2013 spanned DOJ, SEC, OFAC, and
  BIS — see [`../case-studies/fcpa-energy.md`](../case-studies/fcpa-energy.md)).

## Why coordination is the linchpin of polycentric enforcement

Overlapping jurisdiction only *works* if the centres coordinate. Uncoordinated
overlap yields gaps and double-punishment; coordinated overlap yields
**targeted redundancy** — the same design principle argued in
[`../institutional/polycentric-governance.md`](../institutional/polycentric-governance.md).
The trend across the last decade — joint resolutions, penalty crediting,
information-sharing — is the enforcement system *learning to be polycentric on
purpose* rather than by accident.
