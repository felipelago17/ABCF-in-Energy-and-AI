# Steinberg & Ramirez — The SEC's Neglected Weapon (§ 17(a) and loss-avoidance)

**Citation:** Marc I. Steinberg & Arturo J. Carrillo Ramirez,
*The SEC's Neglected Weapon: A Proposed Amendment to Section 17(a)(3) of the
Securities Act to Reach Negligent Insider Trading*, 19 U. Pa. J. Bus. L. 239
(2017).

> **Educational summary — verify against the article before relying on it.**
> Attribution of specific arguments should be confirmed against the published
> text; the authors' framing is paraphrased here.

## Why it is in this collection

This article supplies two threads the module's daily monitor operationalizes:

1. **Loss-avoidance as the dominant classical-theory scenario.** The authors
   document that trading to **avoid a loss** — selling on undisclosed bad news
   ahead of a price decline — is the **most common classical-theory** insider
   trading fact pattern, even though it is easy to under-count because the trader
   books no positive gain. This is the empirical/ doctrinal backing for treating
   loss-avoidance as a first-class category (see the module's
   [Core Concepts](../README.md#the-loss-avoidance-doctrine)).

2. **Securities Act § 17(a) as an under-used enforcement tool.** The article
   argues the SEC has neglected **§ 17(a)** — particularly its
   **negligence-based** limbs — as a vehicle for insider-trading enforcement,
   and proposes amending **§ 17(a)(3)** to reach **negligent insider trading**,
   lowering the scienter threshold below the *knowing/reckless* standard required
   under Exchange Act § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5.

## Key points

- **Scienter gap.** § 10(b)/Rule 10b-5 require scienter (intent or recklessness).
  § 17(a)(2) and § 17(a)(3) can be established on a **negligence** showing but
  apply only "in the offer or sale" of securities, limiting their reach.
- **Proposed § 17(a)(3) amendment.** Extend § 17(a)(3) to capture negligent
  insider trading, giving the SEC a lower-scienter "weapon" for cases where
  intent is hard to prove but the informational abuse is clear.
- **Remedial link.** Because a large share of classical-theory cases are
  loss-avoidance trades, and because disgorgement/§ 21A penalties attach to the
  **loss avoided** (see [*SEC v. Antar*](sec-v-antar.md)), a negligence-based
  § 17(a) theory would broaden liability without changing the remedy measure.

## Relationship to the framework

- Justifies the monitor's **§ 17(a) flag**: SEC complaints increasingly pair
  Rule 10b-5 counts with § 17(a) theories, and the negligence limbs are worth
  tracking as an enforcement-posture signal.
- Anchors the monitor's **loss-avoidance** fact-pattern tag in secondary
  scholarship, not just the primary-source remedy cases.
