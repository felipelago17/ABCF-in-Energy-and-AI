# UK Bribery Act 2010

**Summary.** The Bribery Act 2010 (in force 1 July 2011) is broader than the
FCPA in three respects that matter for governance design: it covers
**commercial (private-sector) bribery**, it criminalises **being bribed**, and
— via **Section 7** — it creates a **corporate offence of failing to prevent
bribery** with near-strict liability, subject only to an **adequate procedures**
defence. That last feature makes the Act a *program-forcing* statute: the only
defence a company has is the quality of its own compliance architecture.

## At a glance

| Offence | Section | What it captures |
|---|---|---|
| Bribing another person | s.1 | Offering/promising/giving an advantage to induce improper performance |
| Being bribed | s.2 | Requesting/agreeing to receive/accepting an advantage |
| Bribery of a foreign public official | s.6 | Advantage to a foreign public official to influence them and obtain/retain business |
| Failure of a commercial organisation to prevent bribery | s.7 | Corporate liability for bribery by an **associated person** (s.8) |
| Adequate procedures defence | s.7(2) | Complete defence if the organisation had adequate preventive procedures |

## Section 6 — foreign public officials

Section 6 mirrors the FCPA anti-bribery provision but is drafted differently: it
turns on **intending to influence** a foreign public official in their capacity
and to **obtain or retain business or a business advantage**. "Foreign public
official" is defined in **s.6(4)**. Unlike the FCPA there is **no facilitation-
payments exception** — small "grease" payments that might fall within the narrow
FCPA exception are, in principle, bribes under UK law.

## Section 7 — the corporate "failure to prevent" offence

Section 7 is the Act's signature innovation. A **relevant commercial
organisation** (broadly, any body carrying on a business in any part of the UK,
wherever incorporated) is **liable** where a person **associated** with it
(s.8 — an employee, agent, subsidiary, or anyone performing services for it)
bribes another intending to obtain or retain business or an advantage *for the
organisation*.

Two governance consequences follow:
1. **Jurisdictional reach is wide.** A non-UK company that "carries on a
   business, or part of a business" in the UK can be prosecuted for the conduct
   of an associated person *anywhere in the world*. UK operations or a UK nexus
   pull the whole enterprise into scope.
2. **The only defence is your program.** Under **s.7(2)** it is a defence for
   the organisation to prove — on the balance of probabilities — that it had in
   place **"adequate procedures designed to prevent persons associated with
   [it] from undertaking such conduct."** The burden sits on the defendant.

### The six principles (MoJ statutory guidance)

The Ministry of Justice guidance frames "adequate procedures" around six
principles. These map cleanly onto the repository's program model:
1. **Proportionate procedures** — scaled to bribery risk and organisational size.
2. **Top-level commitment** — board and senior management ownership.
3. **Risk assessment** — periodic, documented, informed by sector/jurisdiction.
4. **Due diligence** — risk-based, on associated persons.
5. **Communication (including training)** — embedded and understood.
6. **Monitoring and review** — the program is tested and updated.

See [`compliance/board-management-certification.md`](../compliance/board-management-certification.md)
and [`risk-tools/abcf-risk-matrix.md`](../risk-tools/abcf-risk-matrix.md).

> **"Adequate" (Bribery Act s.7) vs. "reasonable" (later failure-to-prevent
> offences).** The Bribery Act uses *adequate* procedures. The UK's later
> failure-to-prevent-fraud offence (Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency
> Act 2023) uses *reasonable* procedures — a subtly different standard. Do not
> conflate the two regimes.

## Enforcement: SFO and FCA

- **Serious Fraud Office (SFO)** — investigates and prosecutes serious/complex
  bribery and corruption. The SFO's principal resolution tool is the **Deferred
  Prosecution Agreement (DPA)**, available since 2014 and approved by a judge in
  open court (the **Rolls-Royce** 2017 DPA — £497m UK portion — is the landmark).
- **Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)** — pursues regulated firms for systems-
  and-controls failures (Principle 3 / SYSC), a separate hook from the criminal
  Act. A firm can face FCA action for weak anti-bribery controls even absent a
  proven bribe.

This dual track (criminal SFO + regulatory FCA) is itself a polycentric design:
two authorities with different standards of proof and remedies, covering gaps
each other would miss. See
[`governance-resources/enforcement-coordination.md`](../governance-resources/enforcement-coordination.md).

## Cross-jurisdictional triggers

A company should assume UK Bribery Act exposure where any of these are present:
- UK operations, a UK subsidiary, or "part of a business" carried on in the UK;
- UK-based directors or a board nexus authorising or overseeing the conduct;
- associated persons (agents/distributors/JV partners) acting for the group;
- listing or fundraising activity with a UK footprint.

Because s.7 reaches associated persons globally, UK exposure frequently
**overlaps** with FCPA exposure on the same facts (as in Rolls-Royce and
Glencore, which produced coordinated UK/US/Brazil resolutions). See
[`case-studies/uk-bribery-prosecutions.md`](../case-studies/uk-bribery-prosecutions.md).
